Search for Japanese interior design and you'll get the same page forty times. Seven ancient principles. Wabi-sabi. A photo of a bare room with one bowl in it. The list is always identical, always translated the same way, and never attributed to anyone.
</p>

So I went looking for where the list came from. The answer is more interesting than the list.

### The seven principles are real. They're also from 1971.

*Kanso, fukinsei, shizen, koko, yūgen, datsuzoku, seijaku.* Simplicity, asymmetry, naturalness, weathered austerity, subtle depth, freedom from convention, stillness.

These were named as a set by Hisamatsu Shin'ichi, a philosopher at Kyoto University who was also a trained Zen scholar and tea master, in his book *Zen and the Fine Arts*. The English edition came out through Kodansha International in 1971.

Here's what matters. Hisamatsu wasn't writing a decorating manual and he wasn't inventing a style. He was looking across calligraphy, ink painting, gardens, flower arranging and tea ware, and describing what they had in common. The seven characteristics are a diagnosis, not a recipe. They're what a finished work tends to have when it comes out of a certain quality of attention.

That distinction gets lost the moment the list becomes a blog subheading. "Apply kanso to your living room" isn't wrong exactly, but it inverts the original logic. The simplicity was a symptom. Now it's being sold as the treatment.

The sensibility Hisamatsu described genuinely does go back centuries. The tidy numbered list does not. Both facts can sit in the same sentence, and almost nobody puts them there.

### Wabi-sabi followed a similar route

Wabi-sabi as a Japanese aesthetic idea is old and tied to tea practice. Wabi-sabi as a phrase in Western design conversation is much younger. It's largely credited to Leonard Koren's slim 1994 book *Wabi-Sabi for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers*. Koren says he introduced the term. Other accounts say he helped bring it into Western aesthetic theory, which is the more defensible version. Either way, the vocabulary most English-language decor writing uses arrived through that book.

Worth knowing before you use the word as a shortcut: Wabi-sabi isn't minimalism. Minimalism reduces to reach a clean result. Wabi-sabi accepts wear, asymmetry and age as part of the beauty. A room can be minimal and have nothing wabi-sabi about it. Most showroom-white interiors labelled wabi-sabi are exactly that.

### Ma is not "empty space"

Ma gets translated as negative space, which is close enough to be misleading. It's better understood as the interval. The pause. The gap that gives the things around it their meaning, the same way a rest in music isn't silence but structure.

Practically, that means the empty part of a Japanese room isn't leftover. It's the part doing the work. Furniture sits apart. Walls stay mostly bare. Your eye gets somewhere to rest between objects.

The test isn't how little you own. It's whether the emptiness feels deliberate or just unfinished.

## Now the physical stuff—and the part that will save you money

### Tatami mats do not come in one size

This is the single most useful thing to know before buying anything, and I couldn't find a decor page that leads with it.

Every tatami mat holds a 2:1 length-to-width ratio. That part is universal, and it's why mats tile into the classic room layouts. The actual dimensions vary by region, because mat sizes were historically tied to the spacing between structural pillars.

There are four standard sizes:
* **Kyōma** (Kansai, including Kyoto and Osaka): roughly 191 x 95.5 cm (about 1.82 m²)
* **Chūkyōma** (Nagoya region): roughly 182 x 91 cm (about 1.66 m²)
* **Edoma or Kantōma** (Tokyo and the north): roughly 176 x 88 cm (about 1.55 m²)
* **Danchima** (apartment size standardized for public housing in the 1970s–80s): roughly 170 x 85 cm (about 1.45 m²)

| Regional Standard | Primary Region / Origin | Approximate Dimensions | Area per Mat |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Kyōma** | Kansai (Kyoto & Osaka) | 191 × 95.5 cm | ~1.82 m² |

| **Danchima** | Standardized public housing (1970s–80s) | 170 × 85 cm | ~1.45 m² |

Rooms in Japan are described in *jō*, meaning mat count. A listing says 6-jō or 8-jō. Because the mats differ, a 6-jō room in Kyoto is meaningfully bigger than a 6-jō room in Tokyo. Comparing the largest and smallest standards, that's roughly a quarter more floor per mat, which over six mats is about two extra square meters.

If you're buying from a Western retailer, check which standard they're selling. Around 180 x 90 cm is common. Mixing standards in one room means the mats won't tile cleanly.

The mat surface is woven *igusa* rush over a rice-straw or compressed-fiber core. Thickness runs around 5.5 to 6 cm depending on standard.

### Shoji and fusuma are not the same thing

People use these interchangeably and they shouldn't.

* **Shoji** are lattice frames covered in translucent *washi* paper. They let light through while blocking the view, and they usually sit on exterior openings. That soft, even, shadowless light people associate with Japanese rooms comes from shoji.
* **Fusuma** are opaque, papered or cloth-covered panels. They block light completely and divide interior rooms. They're what makes a Japanese floor plan reconfigurable instead of fixed.

> **Practical note:** Shoji paper tears. If you install it in a house with kids or pets, budget for replacing panels periodically. That's normal maintenance in Japan, not a defect.

### Everything sits low

Chabudai low tables, zabuton floor cushions, zaisu legless chairs, futons that fold away during the day. The logic is that a room shouldn't be locked into one function. A space that's a bedroom at night becomes a living room at breakfast because the bedding goes into a closet.

You don't have to go full floor-level to use the idea. Lower your sightlines and leave the walls above eye height alone. That alone changes how a Western room feels.

### Materials stay honest

Wood is stained, not painted, because the grain is the point. Hinoki cypress, sugi cedar, bamboo, paper, straw. Colors stay muted so the materials carry the visual interest.

## If you want to actually do this

Skip the shopping list. Start here:

1. **Clear one horizontal surface completely** and leave it clear for a week. See whether the emptiness reads as calm or as missing something. That tells you more about your tolerance for *ma* than any article will.
2. **Replace one light source with something diffused.** Shoji-style panels, a paper shade, anything that stops throwing hard shadows.
3. **Buy one good object with visible irregularity** rather than five matched ones. Handmade ceramic, unsealed wood, something that will look better in ten years than it does now.
4. **Lower one thing.** A coffee table, a seat, a bed platform.
5. **Then, if you still want it, buy tatami.** One good mat, correct standard, real igusa. Live with it through a full year of your climate before you commit to a whole room.

The order matters. Most people buy the objects first and wonder why the room still doesn't feel right. The feeling comes from what you removed, not what you added.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are the seven principles of Japanese design ancient?
The sensibility is centuries old. The specific list of seven was named by philosopher Hisamatsu Shin'ichi in *Zen and the Fine Arts*, published in English in 1971. He was describing Zen art rather than writing interior design rules.

### Are all tatami mats the same size?
No. All hold a 2:1 ratio, but there are four regional standards ranging from roughly 170 x 85 cm to 191 x 95.5 cm. Check which standard you're buying, because different standards won't tile together.

### What's the difference between shoji and fusuma?
Shoji are translucent washi-paper panels that filter light, usually on exterior openings. Fusuma are opaque panels that divide interior rooms and block light entirely.

### Is wabi-sabi the same as minimalism?
No. Minimalism aims at a clean, resolved result. Wabi-sabi values wear, asymmetry, and age. A room can be minimal without being wabi-sabi at all.

### What does jō mean in a Japanese apartment listing?
It's the number of tatami mats that fit the floor. Because mat sizes vary regionally, the same jō figure describes different floor areas in Tokyo and Kyoto.
