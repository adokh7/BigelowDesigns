<p className="drop-cap text-body-lg text-ink-800 leading-relaxed">For several years, the dominant decorating conversation revolved around restraint. Quiet luxury, soft neutrals, pared-back rooms and carefully controlled palettes offered an appealing sense of calm.</p>

Autumn/winter 2026 is not exactly rejecting that idea. But it is loosening its grip.

Across designer surveys, search data, new-season collections and industry forecasts, a remarkably consistent theme is emerging: homes are becoming more individual, more layered and considerably less interested in looking anonymous.

The American Society of Interior Designers' 2026 Trends Outlook describes a wider move from restraint towards boldness and individuality, including saturated colour and more visible personality. Meanwhile, 1stDibs' ninth annual designer survey found maximalism and eclecticism among the styles designers expect to remain particularly prominent in 2026.

And the shift is now filtering directly into autumn collections. An early look across major autumn 2026 ranges identified burgundy, rust, caramel, dark green, light blue and yellow appearing repeatedly, alongside pattern, mid-century references and richer finishes.

So, while there are plenty of individual trends worth knowing, the real autumn/winter 2026 decorating story is bigger than any particular paint colour or sofa shape.

**Personality is becoming the trend.**

---

## What are the biggest autumn/winter 2026 decorating trends?

The key decorating trends emerging for autumn/winter 2026 are:

* Richer, warmer colour palettes
* Considered maximalism
* Vintage and antique furniture
* Statement wallpaper and pattern
* Tactile, imperfect surfaces
* Nostalgic decorating
* Softer, sculptural shapes
* Playful, unexpected details

What connects them is the move towards homes that look collected rather than prescribed. Here is how each trend is taking shape.

---

### 1. Rich colours are replacing safe neutrals

Perhaps the easiest autumn/winter 2026 shift to spot is colour.

Chocolate brown remains particularly important. In 1stDibs' survey of 468 design professionals, 33% identified chocolate brown as a leading colour for 2026, while designers broadly anticipated earthy and dramatic browns, greens and reds.

Retail evidence suggests those deeper shades are carrying straight into autumn. Retail previews of AW26 collections highlight recurring appearances of burgundy, rust, caramel, dark green, yellow and light blue across major ranges.

Houzz search behaviour points in a similar direction, recording substantial increases in searches for "rust colors" and "chocolate brown", alongside continued interest in sage, taupe, cream and mushroom shades.

The important part is not that neutrals have suddenly disappeared. They haven't. Instead, neutrals are becoming warmer and are increasingly being used alongside shades that give a room greater depth. Think chocolate brown with pale blue. Burgundy with warm timber. Rust beside butter yellow. Forest green against cream.

> **How to use it at home:** You do not need to repaint an entire room. Start with one darker anchor: a lacquered side table, velvet chair, painted cabinet or large rug. Then repeat that colour once or twice elsewhere so it feels intentional rather than accidental.

---

### 2. Maximalism is becoming more considered

Maximalism is undoubtedly part of the 2026 story, but calling the trend simply "more is more" misses what has changed.

In the 1stDibs survey, 39% of participating designers identified maximalism as a style expected to remain popular in 2026, while 38% selected eclecticism. Designer Brittany Giannone described contemporary maximalism to 1stDibs as layering that creates warmth, depth and a personal narrative rather than accumulating things purely for abundance.

That distinction matters. The newest maximalist rooms are less interested in filling every available inch. They are interested in putting meaningful things together.

A striped chair might sit beside an antique table. Contemporary art might hang over traditional wallpaper. A modern sofa might share the room with inherited ceramics, travel finds and books accumulated over years. It is maximalism with an editor.

### 3. The 'collected home' is taking over

Closely related to maximalism is one of the most useful ideas emerging this year: the collected home. Rather than furnishing a room as one complete look, the approach encourages spaces to evolve gradually.

According to 1stDibs, an average of 36% of items sourced by surveyed designers for projects were vintage or antique, the highest proportion recorded since 2021. Eighty-five per cent of respondents had sourced vintage pieces, while the reported use of pre-1920 antiques also increased.

Contemporary design coverage reflects the same shift, emphasizing mixing textures, warmer tones and vintage-inspired pieces rather than creating rooms that appear to have been purchased all at once. Old timber, worn leather, aged brass, vintage textiles, ceramic lamps and framed artwork introduce something a perfectly coordinated new furniture set struggles to reproduce: history.

> **The best rule to follow:** Do not try to make something look vintage. Use something that actually has age, character or meaning whenever possible.

---

### 4. Wallpaper is getting braver

Wallpaper never really disappeared, but autumn/winter 2026 looks set to make it much harder to ignore.

Designer surveys show strong enthusiasm for wallpaper, including using it across whole rooms and extending it onto ceilings. That reflects a wider rejection of the idea that patterned surfaces need to be confined to one cautious "feature wall".

Floral papers, stripes, heritage motifs and scenic designs all fit neatly into the more expressive mood of the year. The most contemporary approach is often surprisingly traditional: rather than treating wallpaper as the room's single statement, let it form the backdrop and continue layering.

---

### 5. Texture is becoming as important as colour

Not every personality-filled interior needs to be brightly coloured. For quieter homes, texture may be the more important autumn/winter 2026 tool.

Houzz search analysis points towards growing interest in tactile and characterful surfaces, including scalloped tile, terracotta flooring, limewash and wainscoting. Plaster that shows movement, wood with visible grain, nubby upholstery, woven lampshades, and handmade tiles give restraint a personality of its own.

### 6. Nostalgia is getting cooler

Another defining AW26 idea is the return of things we were once told were dated: traditional furniture, skirted upholstery, china cabinets, decorative trims, florals, dark timber, and old-fashioned lighting.

House Beautiful recently described the emerging "granny cool" aesthetic as an evolution of cottagecore, combining antiques and craftsmanship with richer colours, stronger pattern and more sculptural elements. Homes & Gardens highlighted renewed interest in nostalgic elements such as coloured glassware, china cabinets and wainscoting.

The appeal lies in taking recognisable elements and disrupting them. A traditional floral sofa can sit beneath contemporary art. A mahogany cabinet can work in a room painted pale blue. Chintz might be paired with chrome.

### 7. Furniture is losing its hard edges

Curves have been gaining ground for several seasons, but current search data suggests the idea is expanding beyond rounded sofas and chairs into architectural details.

Searches for scalloped tile and wave tile have surged. Scalloped edges, wavy mirrors, rounded tables and softly irregular objects make rooms feel less rigid and look chosen rather than default.

---

### 8. Decorating is getting a sense of humour

Interiors are becoming slightly less serious. Pinterest's 2026 Predicts report anticipates growing interest in circus-inspired interiors and nonconformity.

That does not mean everyone is about to install a striped circus ceiling. What matters is a willingness to decorate with wit: an unexpectedly shaped lamp, a striped ceiling, an unusual ceramic found at a flea market, or an artwork that makes visitors smile.

---

### 9. Imperfection is becoming an asset

Highly coordinated spaces can look beautiful, but they can also look strangely untouched. The emerging alternative values irregular finishes, mixed periods, visibly handmade objects and arrangements that feel as though they developed naturally.

Houzz's trend outlook describes homeowners prioritising warmth, longevity and homes that support real life rather than simply chasing novelty. In many cases, the most effective thing you can add to an interior is something you already own but previously thought was too odd, too old or too mismatched to display.

---

## Does this mean minimalism is over?

No. That is one of the places where trend forecasting can easily become exaggerated.

Evidence clearly points towards stronger interest in maximalism, colour, vintage pieces, pattern and individuality, but it does not establish that minimalist interiors are disappearing. Organic modernism remains prominent.

Impersonal minimalism is losing its authority. A minimalist home can still feel completely current when the few things inside it have character: an extraordinary chair, hand-thrown pottery, interesting timber, or art chosen because its owner loves it.

---

## How to make your home feel more personal this autumn

1. **Look at what you already have:** Bring an inherited piece out of storage, frame something meaningful, or move art to an unexpected wall.
2. **Mix intentionally:** Mix furniture you previously thought did not "go".
3. **Choose genuine color:** Introduce one colour you genuinely love rather than one an algorithm keeps showing you.
4. **Add slowly:** Build through layers, vintage pieces, and objects that carry a story.

---

## The real autumn/winter 2026 trend? Stop trying to look on-trend

There is an amusing contradiction at the centre of autumn/winter 2026 decorating: the biggest trend is essentially not looking as though you followed a trend.

Pick the elements that actually feel like you. Because this autumn, a room that reveals something about the person who lives there is far more desirable than one that reveals how closely they followed the latest decorating rules.
