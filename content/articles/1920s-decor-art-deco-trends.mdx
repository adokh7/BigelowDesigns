# Americans Search 1920s Decor More Than They Search 2026 Trends

Here's what fell out of a US home decor keyword export I pulled for this piece.

Add up the searches for decade-specific decor on Google—1920s, 1930s, 1940s, all the way through the 90s—and you get roughly 6,000 a month. Add up every search for current decor trends, including "home decor trends 2026," and you get about 2,400.

Roughly two and a half to one, in favour of the past.

And one decade beats every single trend term on its own. "Home decor 1920s" pulls about 1,600 a month. No trend query in the dataset comes close.

Those are rounded search bands rather than exact counts, so treat the ratio as direction rather than arithmetic. But the direction is clear, and it's the opposite of what the entire trend-content industry is built on.

---

## There's a real reason, and it isn't the whole reason

Something genuinely happened to the 1920s.

The style we call Art Deco takes its name from the *Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes*, held in Paris from 28 April 1925 and running roughly six months. It drew somewhere around sixteen million visitors. It was where the style was first shown at scale, and France used it to reassert itself as the world's arbiter of taste after the economic wreckage of the First World War.

That was a hundred years ago, and the anniversary was marked properly. The Musée des Arts Décoratifs in Paris ran "1925–2025. One Hundred Years of Art Deco" from 22 October 2025 to 26 April 2026.

Worth flagging: I found a widely-shared blog listing that exhibition as ending in February. It didn't. The museum's own page says 26 April, and a second independent source confirms it. Check dates against the institution, not against decor blogs.

So the centenary is a tidy explanation for the 1920s lead. It's also not sufficient, and I'd rather say that than sell you the neat version.

The second-most searched decade is the 1960s, at around 1,300. Third is the 1990s, at around 1,000. Neither has a centenary. Whatever is pulling people toward decade-specific decor is broader than one anniversary. The export is a single snapshot with no time series, so I can't show whether 1920s interest rose after the exhibition opened.

What I can say is that people search for decades with a consistency that current-year trend content doesn't come close to matching.

## The thing almost every decor article gets wrong

Art Deco is not a 1920s term.

Nobody at that 1925 exposition was designing "Art Deco," because the phrase didn't exist. It was coined by historians in the 1960s, retrofitted from the exposition's full name—*Arts Décoratifs*.

That's not a technicality from an obscure source. The Victoria & Albert Museum states it in its own collection notes, and the National Building Museum states it independently in an interview with the Art Deco Society of Washington. Two institutions, no shared upstream, same conclusion.

There's a further wrinkle worth knowing: the style went out of favour and wasn't widely celebrated again until the 1980s. So the sequence runs: built in the 1920s, ignored, named in the 1960s, revived in the 1980s, searched heavily in 2026.

And the definition itself is shakier than the confident guides suggest. Mathurin Jonchères, an assistant curator at the Musée des Arts Décoratifs, has said it's extremely difficult to give a strict definition of Art Deco—there was no manifesto, no theoreticians declaring what it should be, and the movement is full of contradictions. The V&A puts it similarly: arguably not one style, but a pastiche of sources and influences.

Anyone handing you seven clean rules for Art Deco is describing something tidier than the thing itself.

## What the 1920s actually looked like

The material list from the period is more varied than the gold-and-black shorthand suggests: jade, silver, ivory, chrome, and—this is the part that gets left out—plastics. The exposition deliberately put handcrafted luxury next to mass-produced goods. That was the argument: modern manufacturing and elegance could coexist.

The forms are the part that survived: clean geometry, streamlining, stylised rather than naturalistic ornament.

If you want the actual objects rather than a mood board, the MAD exhibition featured André Groult's shagreen chest of drawers and Pierre Chareau's desk-library from the 1925 French Embassy pavilion, reinstalled. The V&A holds Robert Bonfils' original exposition poster in its permanent collection, which you can view online.

## Why this connects to right now

There's a reason 1920s interest doesn't feel out of step with 2026.

Benjamin Moore named **Silhouette AF-655**—an espresso brown—its 2026 Color of the Year, announced in October 2025. Its 2025 colour, Cinnamon Slate, was brown-family too. Two consecutive years of deep, warm, saturated neutrals from the largest name in American paint.

Deep browns, chrome, geometry, warm metals: the 1920s palette and the 2026 palette are not strangers.

I'd stop short of claiming one caused the other. But if you're wondering whether decade-referencing decor will look dated next year, the colour direction suggests otherwise.

## Two more things in the data

* **Nobody agrees on money:** The obvious story is that shoppers want cheap. The data won't cooperate. "Home decor affordable" pulls 40,500 and "home decor for cheap" 12,100—but "home decor luxury" pulls 33,100 and "luxury home décor" another 27,100, at roughly double the cost-per-click. Both audiences are large. The decor internet mostly writes for one and pretends the other doesn't exist.
* **3D printing is quietly there:** "3d printed home decor," "home decor 3d printing" and "home decor 3d print" together pull around 2,500 a month across platforms. It's small, but almost no decor publication covers it at all, which is unusual for a category with that much search interest.

## A methodology warning, free of charge

If you work with keyword exports in this niche, check your accents.

This dataset lists "home decor" and "home décor" as separate keywords with identical volumes. "Home décor ideas" and "home decor ideas" each report 301,000—that's one query counted twice. Normalise the *é* and deduplicate, and total volume drops from about 3.3 million to about 2.74 million.

The raw file overstates by roughly 20%. Anyone summing this export without normalising is reporting a market a fifth bigger than it is.

## If you want to use a decade

* **Pick the forms, not the props:** Geometry, streamlining, and stylised ornament are what carried a century. A gold flapper-era wall decal is costume.
* **Check what's actually verifiable:** Museum collection records are free, searchable, and show real objects rather than blog repetition.
* **Anchor with colour first:** Deep espresso browns and warm metals do more structural work than any single accent piece.
* **One decade, one room:** Borrowing heavily from four decades at once rarely reads as eclectic; it usually reads as unresolved.

## Frequently asked questions

### When was Art Deco named?
In the 1960s. Historians coined the term retrospectively from the name of the 1925 Paris Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes. Nobody working in the 1920s called it Art Deco.

### What years was Art Deco?
The style emerged in western Europe during the 1910s and 1920s and dominated the interwar years. It fell out of favour afterwards and wasn't widely celebrated again until the 1980s.

### What defines 1920s home decor?
Clean geometric shapes, streamlined forms, and stylised ornament, in materials including chrome, silver, jade, ivory, rich woods, and early plastics.

### Is Art Deco coming back in 2026?
Search interest in 1920s decor currently exceeds interest in current-year trend queries. The centenary and the shift towards rich, warm brown palettes make it feel remarkably contemporary.
