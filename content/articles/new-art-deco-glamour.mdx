We spent a solid decade in linen slipcovers and pale oak. It was calming for a while, and then it started to feel like every living room was the same living room. Clients kept using the same word without prompting: flat. So it's not really a surprise that glamour is having a moment again. Just not the glamour you'd expect if your only reference is a 1920s movie set.

The original Art Deco movement came out of Paris and New York in the 1920s and '30s, and it was loud on purpose: gold leaf, black lacquer, sunburst motifs, rooms built to be photographed at a party. What's happening now borrows the bones of that era, the geometry, the materials, the reflective surfaces, and drops the theatrics. Call it restrained Deco. It reads as considered rather than costumed, and that distinction is the whole trick to pulling it off in a real home instead of a stage set.

## Where the geometry actually lives

Deco has always been about repetition and symmetry: clean, structural shapes that repeat rather than one big decorative gesture. The mistake people make trying to bring this into a modern room is reaching for a busy geometric wallpaper and calling it done. That reads as pattern, not architecture, and it dates fast.

Better to let the geometry live in the furniture itself. A fluted oak sideboard, a reeded vanity front, an arched doorway or mirror frame all carry the rhythm of Deco without shouting about it. Fluted and reeded surfaces have a nice side effect too: the grooves catch light differently as the sun moves through the room, so you get texture and shadow play without adding a single extra object.

## Color: away from beige, toward something with weight

The neutral, beige-and-cream palette that's dominated for years is giving ground to color that actually commits: emerald, deep sapphire, burnt ochre, the kind of jewel tones that read as intentional rather than accidental. These aren't colors you sprinkle around casually. They work as anchors: one deeply saturated element in a room that's otherwise fairly quiet.

The material carries as much weight as the color. Heavyweight cotton velvet or a dense mohair on a pair of accent chairs adds real physical presence. You can see the difference in how the fabric catches light before you even sit down. Set against plain white walls and simple, contemporary art, that combination reads as luxurious rather than dated, which is really the whole balancing act of doing this trend well.

## Metal as punctuation, not decoration

No Deco room works without something that catches light: polished brass, warm bronze, brushed nickel. These metals aren't just finish choices, they're doing a job: drawing the eye to the pieces you actually want noticed, like an architectural detail or a well-shaped chair.

The failure mode here is matching everything. A brass lamp, brass hardware, a brass mirror frame, and brass legs on the coffee table all in the same room reads as a showroom set, not a home. Pick one genuinely good piece, a sconce flanking a fireplace, a coffee table with a slim bronze edge, and let it do the work alone. Treat metal like jewelry: one great piece says more than five matching ones.

## The room-by-room version

In a living room, this usually means one or two committed pieces rather than a full redecoration: a pair of velvet chairs, a fluted console, a single arched mirror. You're not trying to convert the whole space; you're adding enough weight that the room stops feeling generic.

A powder room is genuinely the best place to go further, because it's small and you walk through it rather than live in it. This is where a fully saturated wall color, a fluted vanity, and a brass faucet can coexist without feeling like too much, because the scale of the room absorbs the drama that would overwhelm a living room.

Dining rooms respond well to the geometry angle specifically. A stepped-profile light fixture over the table, chairs with a subtle fan-back shape, a rug with a restrained geometric border all read as occasion without needing a single jewel-toned surface.

## Lighting is doing more work than people think

Deco rooms depend on reflection, which means lighting placement matters more here than in a flat, matte room. A step-profile sconce, the kind with layered, tiered shades, throws light in bands that echo the architectural lines elsewhere in the room. An arched mirror opposite a window doubles whatever natural light you have and bounces it back into the space.

Keep the color temperature warm, in the 2700K range. Cool white light flattens brass and makes velvet look synthetic instead of rich, which undercuts the whole point of using these materials in the first place.

## How this differs from the 1920s original

| | 1920s Original | 2026 Version |
|---|---|---|
| Palette | Black, gold, cream, often applied broadly | One saturated jewel tone as an anchor, rest kept quiet |
| Pattern | Bold geometric prints across walls and floors | Geometry built into furniture form, not surface pattern |
| Metal | Ornate, often gilded, applied liberally | One or two restrained pieces per room |
| Scale | Grand, theatrical, built for entertaining | Domestic, livable, built for daily use |
| Overall feel | Costume-accurate revival | A few Deco-adjacent pieces in an otherwise modern room |

## Keeping it from tipping into a theme

The line between "considered" and "costume party" is thinner than people expect, and the way to stay on the right side of it is contrast. Never go full Deco in one room. If you bring in a geometric sideboard, pair it with a plain plaster lamp and simple art, not a matching set of ornate accessories. If you hang an arched brass mirror, put it above a console with clean, unfussy lines rather than another period piece.

That mix, one or two confident, historically-rooted pieces sitting inside an otherwise modern room, is what makes this trend feel current instead of like a rerun. It also means you're not locked in. If jewel tones fall out of favor in a few years, you've got two chairs to reupholster, not an entire room to gut.

## Common questions

**Is Art Deco expensive to pull off?**
Not necessarily. The trend leans on a small number of considered pieces rather than wall-to-wall pattern, so a single well-chosen chair or light fixture can carry a room. Save the spending for the one or two pieces that matter and keep everything else simple. Secondhand and vintage sources are often the better route here, too. An original piece of Deco-era furniture, refinished, tends to have better proportions and craftsmanship than most new reproductions at the same price point.

**Can this work in a rental?**
Yes, more easily than most trends. Furniture, lighting, and textiles do almost all the work here. Nothing requires structural changes or permanent fixtures. A velvet chair, a brass floor lamp, and an arched mirror will get you most of the way there.

**What's the easiest single change to make?**
Lighting. Swapping a plain overhead fixture for something with a stepped or tiered profile, in warm light, changes the character of a room more than almost any other single purchase, and it's the detail most people notice without being able to say why.
